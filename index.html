Sonic Plunge

An automated multi-sensory installation

The Sonic Plunge experience blends trance-inducing sound, video, scent, and haptics into a fully automated deep-dive meditation that feels part dreamscape, part sensory joyride.

𝄀 𝄂

𝄞 Concept

Sonic Plunge is a multi-sensory, sound-led installation—far more than an automated sequence of singing bowls. It is a time-based experience in which sound, light, vibration, and subtle sensory cues unfold as a unified whole.

Like a sound bath, its impact is rooted in full-body perception: spatial sound, felt vibration, low light, and the gradual settling of attention. Spatial audio, physically activated singing bowls, integrated visuals, tactile feedback, and optional scent are all calibrated to support the listening, never compete with it.

This is not a display of technology, but a focused, evolving environment designed to invite immersive, embodied listening.

𝄀 𝄂

♭ Problem

A compelling live sound bath depends on the right space, timing, and facilitator—conditions that are rarely available on demand.

This raises a central question: can that same depth of slow, full-body listening be translated into a system that can be refined, repeated, and accessed more freely—without losing its integrity?

Automation expands access, but it also risks flattening the experience. The challenge is maintaining coherence of timing, tone, and sensory balance so the environment still feels immersive and intentional, rather than mechanical.

𝄀 𝄂

♯ Approach

Sonic Plunge is conceived as a single, integrated environment. Spatial sound and automated singing bowls operate alongside visuals, lighting, scent, and tactile vibration, with tight synchronization so the space is experienced as a unified whole.

Spatial audio is rendered through a wavefield synthesis (WFS) array, allowing sound to occupy physical space rather than collapse into stereo. In parallel, quartz singing bowls are driven by automated exciters, producing resonance through real mechanical energy—not simply playback.

Visuals, lighting, scent, and tactile feedback provide additional layers of atmosphere. These elements are coordinated to remain responsive to the sound, reinforcing the experience without pulling focus from it.

Audio and show control systems are linked over a shared network, enabling precise timing and cohesive transitions. The system is designed for both repeatability and flexibility, with enough resilience to maintain continuity as components evolve.

The soundscapes are designed by Monique Mead, drawing on established sound meditation practices and research-informed protocols shown to reduce stress and support overall well-being. At every stage, decisions return to listening; the technical framework serves the integrity of the experience.

Sonic Plunge is our working title for this build — centered on quartz singing bowls and the systems around them — WFS playback, bowl exciters, DMX and projectors, tactile feeds, scent hardware when it is online. Concept and Approach spell out the architecture; this section is the bench-level mess.

This collaborative effort combined the best of CMU’s artistic brains with robotics brains. Multiple repeats, fine-tuning the whole experience so that the two seamlessly integrated together to provide the best possible experience.

♭ Materials & orchestration

  • Acoustic Quartz singing bowls powered by coded magic mushrooms, automated mallets.
  • Electronics Microcontroller-based control, breadboard circuits, servos or small motors for strike experiments, batteries and regulated power where needed.
  • Light Monitors surrounding the chair to immerse the user in nature.
  • Sensory Tactile transducers, seating vibration, or other physical channels so the low end is not only in your ears — tuned so it supports the bath rather than rattling the room.
  • Software Max/MSP patches for spatial audio and bowl or exciter control; TouchDesigner for projections, DMX lighting, scent cues, and tactile routing; OSC between subsystems so scenes stay locked in time.

𝄐 Timeline

Six stages in order: reference listen, hands-on technique, system definition, parallel spatial audio work, composition and capture, then full integration — bowls, WFS, visuals, and tactile paths in one rehearsal loop.

  1. 1 · Experiencing the sound bath at the studio

    We went to a real sound bath first. Reading about bowls is one thing; feeling low light, slow movement, and sound moving around the room is another. It gave us a shared baseline for level, pacing, and how much silence belongs between gestures — the stuff you do not get from a stereo demo track.

  2. 2 · Trying the bowls ourselves

    On campus we put quartz bowls on the floor and learned the physical technique — soft contact, steady motion, how the mallet meets the rim and outer curve. Awkward at first, which helped: you only feel how responsive the bowl is when your own hand is on the mallet.

  3. 3 · Understanding what needed to be automated

    After listening and playing things by hand, we could finally say what had to run without us standing there for every cue. Two jobs mattered most.

    • Automate the sensory experience, letting scents out according to what we need.
    • Automate the mallet hitting the bowl with enough speed.

  4. 4 · Building the sound design in parallel

    In parallel with the live bowls, we created the spatial experience. With an array of 64 speakers for Wave Field Synthesis, we could fully immerse the audience within a setting, whether that be a lush forest, a serene ocean, or an abstract space. Programming up a full sound experience, all the focus was in ensuring the sound effects and prerecorded instruments sound just as good as if it were live, as well as play along a totally natural timeline, designing sound that would fill up the rest of the space.

  5. 5 · Writing and recording the music

    We wrote and recorded music for the video — straight to picture, then mixed and mastered so it would hold up on the room’s speakers.

    The last step was getting those tracks and the video files ready for playback: formats and levels suited to the projectors and the speaker array for the show.

  6. 6 · Bringing the system together

    The last step was wiring everything so it behaved as one show: the bowls, the speakers in the room, the video, the lights, the vibration, and the scent all starting and changing together instead of each piece on its own. It took a lot of trial runs, but that was the point — one experience, not separate gadgets.

𝄌 Coda — what we’re learning

Looking back, creating this sensory experience was an enormous amount of fun. For several of us it meant stepping into work we had never done before—exploring the musical and listening side of the project while bringing whatever technical chops we already had—and finding that combination deeply rewarding.

What surprised us was how much those worlds fed each other. The same skills that help in one domain could completely change how we approached another—confidence borrowed from code or robotics showing up in aesthetic decisions, and musical intuition sharpening choices we would usually treat as purely engineering.

The trade went both ways: expertise in areas we are strong in helped cover ground where someone else was still learning, and the gaps flipped just as often the next week. Overall, it was an experience that was extremely worth it. Exploring the musical world through tech was something we would go back and do again.
