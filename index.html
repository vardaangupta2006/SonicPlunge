Sonic Plunge

An automated singing bowl sound bath — built for our university music gen ed course.

We are exploring how resonance, light, and electronics can meet in one slow, immersive listening experience: part sonic art, part hands-on engineering, part shared experiment.

Project overview

Sonic Plunge is a semester-long exploration: how to invite people into a calm, sustained listening experience using acoustic objects, light, and carefully timed electronics — without losing the bowls’ natural voice.

Setting

Music gen ed · CMU studio

Built in shared music-technology studio time with access to floorspace, carts, and room to make noise responsibly.

Medium

Quartz bowls + circuits

Frosted singing bowls for long decays; microcontrollers and actuators to explore repeatable, gentle strikes.

Aim

Immersive sound bath

Slow arcs of tone — closer to a meditative field than a song with verses and choruses.

Status

Active prototype

We iterate in the open: document listens, revise wiring, and chase gestures that feel kind in the room.

Sonic Plunge is our working title for a sound-bath-inspired installation centered on quartz singing bowls. The aim is to choreograph tone and texture over time — less like a performance with a fixed score, and more like a slowly shifting acoustic field you can step into.

“Automated” here means we are experimenting with ways to strike or excite the bowls and support them with electronics, so the listening experience can unfold on its own while staying musically sensitive. We are still prototyping: listening, adjusting, and learning what the bowls want to do in the room.

Practically, that has meant breadboards beside bowls, laptops open to sketches and tests, and lots of floor time deciding what counts as “musical” when a motor taps metal. We keep notes on signal paths, strike heights, and room placement so each revision is easier to compare by ear.

The project sits at the overlap of artistic intention, musical listening, and electrical tinkering — building something fragile and beautiful from wires, code, and patience.

Materials & ingredients

  • Acoustic Multiple frosted singing bowls, soft mallets, occasional gong / room mics for reference recordings.
  • Electronics Microcontroller-based control, breadboard circuits, servos or small motors for strike experiments, batteries and regulated power where needed.
  • Light LED accents inside or under bowls to reinforce slow visual rhythm alongside sound.
  • Software Laptop tools for timing tests, audio capture, and iteration notes — whatever helps us repeat a listen honestly.

Build journey

A loose timeline of where we have been — not a spec sheet, just the shape of the work so far.

  1. Concept + early planning

    We aligned on a sound-bath frame: slow time, long tones, and a room that feels held rather than rushed. Sketches and conversations before anything was plugged in.

    We listed adjectives we wanted listeners to use afterward — “spacious,” “warm,” “unhurried” — and rejected ideas that would turn the bowls into a novelty demo. The goal was an environment, not a gadget showcase.

  2. Testing singing bowls

    Hours on the floor with mallets and bowls — learning how each bowl speaks, how it blooms, and what kinds of gestures feel musical instead of mechanical.

    We compared rim tones vs. side strikes, how close mallets could get before the sound turned brittle, and how long to let a note decay before the next gesture — all before automation entered the picture.

  3. Electrical setup

    Microcontrollers, breadboards, and laptop screens joined the bowls. The goal was a modest, reliable loop: try a circuit, listen, break it gently, try again.

    Early wins were boring in a good way: LEDs behaving, servos moving predictably, and a shared language for “PWM,” “ground,” and “that click is the connector, not the bowl.”

  4. Wiring + actuation experiments

    Small motors, improvised arms, tape, and patience — chasing a strike that feels intentional rather than abrupt. Lots of failure, a little comedy, and occasional breakthroughs.

    We chased a strike that reads as a touch, not a knock: adjusting arm length, padding, angle, and delay so the bowl sings instead of complaining. Every ugly attempt still taught us something about weight and timing.

  5. Sound testing + documentation

    We record listens, compare takes, and capture photos so future-us remembers what changed between versions — including the messy middle.

    Documentation is part of the instrument: naming files by date, keeping photos of cable runs, and writing down settings so we can return to a good take without relying on memory alone.

  6. Iteration and refinement

    The project is still in motion — tightening details, reconsidering gestures, and asking what “automated” should feel like when the room is quiet.

    Open questions: how much variation belongs in a “phrase,” how lighting tracks sound without stealing attention, and what minimum viable reliability looks like before we call it shareable with a class.

What we are learning

Prototyping together has meant translating half-finished ideas across tables, laptops, and voice memos. We have gotten better at naming what we hear, not just whether it “works” — a small shift that changes how we iterate.

Connecting artistic intention to physical systems is slower than drawing a diagram. The bowls reward care; the electronics reward patience. When those two line up, the room changes in a way that is hard to screenshot.

Testing has been a kind of listening: repeating setups until we trust them, and staying honest when something feels clever but not kind. We are still learning how to carry an immersive sound experience out of imagination and into a real space — one version at a time.

If there is a single takeaway for the course portfolio, it might be this: the “installation” is not only the objects on the floor — it is also the process of listening together, failing in public, and revising until the room feels a little more generous than when we started.
