Sonic Plunge

An automated multi-sensory installation

The Sonic Plunge experience blends trance-inducing sound, video, scent, and haptics into a fully automated deep-dive meditation that feels part dreamscape, part sensory joyride.

𝄀 𝄂

𝄞 Concept

Sonic Plunge is a multi-sensory, sound-led installation—far more than an automated sequence of singing bowls. It is a time-based experience in which sound, light, vibration, and subtle sensory cues unfold as a unified whole.

Like a sound bath, its impact is rooted in full-body perception: spatial sound, felt vibration, low light, and the gradual settling of attention. Spatial audio, physically activated singing bowls, integrated visuals, tactile feedback, and optional scent are all calibrated to support the listening, never compete with it.

This is not a display of technology, but a focused, evolving environment designed to invite immersive, embodied listening.

𝄀 𝄂

♭ Problem

A compelling live sound bath depends on the right space, timing, and facilitator—conditions that are rarely available on demand.

This raises a central question: can that same depth of slow, full-body listening be translated into a system that can be refined, repeated, and accessed more freely—without losing its integrity?

Automation expands access, but it also risks flattening the experience. The challenge is maintaining coherence of timing, tone, and sensory balance so the environment still feels immersive and intentional, rather than mechanical.

𝄀 𝄂

♯ Approach

We are treating Sonic Plunge as one integrated installation: spatial sound and automated bowls on one control path, visuals, lighting, scent, and tactile vibration on another, with tight synchronization so the room reads as a single responsive environment.

Sound and bowls. Spatial audio is rendered through a wavefield synthesis (WFS) speaker array so energy has a place in the room instead of collapsing to stereo “inside your head.” Alongside that, we are driving quartz singing bowls with automated exciters (we prototype using what we call the “Musical Mushroom” units) so the metal rings from real mechanical energy — not only a recording of a strike played back on a cone.

Show and senses. Real-time or generative projections, DMX-controlled lighting, automated scent release, and tactile transducers in seating give us separate handles on atmosphere. The aim is audio-reactive visuals and lighting, with scent and low-frequency vibration underscoring what you hear, so the story stays embodied instead of only visual.

How the pieces talk. In our design, audio control (WFS plus bowl exciters, largely in Max/MSP) and show control (TouchDesigner for picture, lights, scent cues, and tactile feeds) stay linked over the network — OSC messages and shared timing — so scenes can shift together. Reliability and flexibility both matter: we need repeatability for rehearsal, headroom for live tweaks, and enough fault tolerance that one subsystem glitch does not flatten the whole arc.

Under all of that is still the same floor work: mounts, grounding, and long listening sessions. The signal chain only earns its keep if the listening says so.

Sonic Plunge is our working title for this build — centered on quartz singing bowls and the systems around them — WFS playback, bowl exciters, DMX and projectors, tactile feeds, scent hardware when it is online. Concept and Approach spell out the architecture; this section is the bench-level mess.

“Automated” still means hours of manual tuning — the difference is whether the hand is on a mallet or a fader. We log strikes, capture OSC routes, and compare listens so we know what changed between takes.

♭ Materials & orchestration

  • Acoustic Multiple frosted singing bowls, soft mallets, occasional gong / room mics for reference recordings — our “strings & choir.”
  • Electronics Microcontroller-based control, breadboard circuits, servos or small motors for strike experiments, batteries and regulated power where needed.
  • Light LED accents inside or under bowls to reinforce slow visual rhythm alongside sound.
  • Sensory Tactile transducers, seating vibration, or other physical channels so the low end is not only in your ears — tuned so it supports the bath rather than rattling the room.
  • Software Max/MSP patches for spatial audio and bowl or exciter control; TouchDesigner for projections, DMX lighting, scent cues, and tactile routing; OSC between subsystems so scenes stay locked in time.

𝄐 Timeline

Six stages in order: reference listen, hands-on technique, system definition, parallel spatial audio work, composition and capture, then full integration — bowls, WFS, visuals, and tactile paths in one rehearsal loop.

  1. 1 · Experiencing the sound bath at the studio

    We went to a real sound bath first. Reading about bowls is one thing; feeling low light, slow movement, and sound moving around the room is another. It gave us a shared baseline for level, pacing, and how much silence belongs between gestures — the stuff you do not get from a stereo demo track.

    It also sharpened the brief: this was never “make it loud.” Pressure in the air, vibration through the floor or seat, and where your eyes rest matter as much as frequency content — exactly the mix we later tried to rebuild with WFS, exciters, lighting, and tactile paths.

  2. 2 · Trying the bowls ourselves

    On campus we put bowls on the floor and learned the physical technique — soft contact, steady motion, where the mallet lands on the wall. Awkward at first, which helped: you only see how sensitive the metal is when your own hand is on the mallet.

    Tiny changes in angle, speed, or pressure changed the partials more than any EQ move. That set the bar for what “good” meant once we swapped the hand for an exciter: repeatable energy into the bowl, not a sample with reverb slapped on top.

  3. 3 · Understanding what needed to be automated

    After reference listening and manual playing, we wrote down what the installation actually had to do: long decays, enough silence, spatial motion through the WFS array, physically excited bowls (not only playback), lighting and visuals that track the same arc, and tactile or scent layers that stay in sync.

    That turned observation into design decisions: which problems belong to Max/MSP and the speaker grid, which belong to TouchDesigner and DMX, and where OSC ties them together. Not every gesture became a motor, but we finally had a spec we could test instead of vibe-checking alone.

  4. 4 · Building the sound design in parallel

    In parallel with bowl prototyping, we stood up the spatial side: WFS array tuning, panning and distance cues, and multichannel material that could share the room with live bowl energy instead of masking it. The question was always whether the speakers and the metal sounded like one field.

    This stretch was less about “correct” in a mastering sense and more about shaping an environment — what you notice lying on the floor for twenty minutes when the array is doing its job.

  5. 5 · Writing and recording the music

    We wrote sketches on paper and in the DAW, then recorded passes through the same speaker layout we were iterating for WFS — same room, same rough geometry, so we were not fooling ourselves with headphones only.

    The test was always compositional sound against physical bowl strikes: what needed more air, what fought the transients from the exciters, and what still felt kind after a long day. Plenty of ideas died once they met the real bowls.

  6. 6 · Bringing the system together

    Integration meant running partial show files end to end — Max/MSP driving WFS and exciters, TouchDesigner carrying picture, DMX, scent, and tactile feeds, OSC keeping the clocks honest. Ugly runs at first; enough to see whether we had one installation or three demos sharing a power strip.

    We are still chasing noise, latency, and programming bugs, but the goal is a single synchronized system: bowls, spatial audio, visuals, lighting, vibration, and scent cues on one narrative clock instead of independent faders.

𝄌 Coda — what we’re learning

Prototyping has meant dragging half-finished ideas across tables, laptops, and voice memos. We got sharper at naming what we hear — not only whether a patch “works,” but whether it belongs in the same story as the WFS image and the bowl strikes.

Turning a schematic into a room takes longer than drawing it. The bowls reward care; the speaker grid rewards measurement; TouchDesigner rewards patience when a cue drifts by a frame. When those line up, the change is hard to screenshot but easy to feel on the floor.

Testing stays a kind of listening: repeat a run until we trust the sync, then change one variable. We are still learning how to carry a full-room idea from imagination into a lab without sanding off what made it human.

If this page has a thesis, it is that the installation is not the objects alone — it is spatial audio, physically driven bowls, coordinated light and vibration, and the slow work of listening together until the room feels a little more generous than when we started.
