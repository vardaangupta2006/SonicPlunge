Sonic Plunge

An automated singing bowl sound bath we are building at CMU — sound, light, and other sensory pieces in the same system.

We are wiring up bowls, lights, speakers, and tactile or haptic layers where we can route them, so the room reads as a full-body experience — not only what you hear.

𝄀 𝄂

𝄞 Concept

Sonic Plunge is our attempt to recreate the feeling of a sound bath using technology.

A traditional sound bath is not just about hearing sound — it is about how it feels in your body. There is a physical quality to it: the vibrations, the space, the way sound moves around you. We wanted to build something that captures that full experience, not just the audio.

To do that, we are combining robotics, sound design, and a deliberate sensory layer — light, vibration, and spatial audio working together so your body has more than one thing to hold onto. The goal is still immersive and calming, even though it is automated.

𝄀 𝄂

♭ Problem

Sound baths are powerful, but they are not always easy to access. They usually require a specific place, time, and instructor.

We wanted to explore what happens if that experience could be available on demand.

The challenge is that once you automate something like this, it can easily lose what makes it special. So the real problem we are trying to solve is not just accessibility, but how to keep the experience meaningful and immersive even when it is driven by technology.

𝄀 𝄂

♯ Approach

Our approach is to break the experience into its core parts and rebuild it.

On the hardware side, we are using a robotic system to physically strike the singing bowls, so the sound is still being produced in a real, tactile way.

At the same time, we are designing the audio using a multi-speaker setup to control how sound moves through the space. This lets us shape the experience more intentionally.

We are also designing visuals and other sensory elements on purpose — color drift, brightness, touch or vibration where it fits — so the installation is not only “correct” but felt in more than one sense at once.

The project sits somewhere between engineering and art. It is not just about making it work — it is about making it feel right.

♪ At a glance

The short version: quartz bowls, borrowed studio time, and a pile of boards and motors we keep apologizing to when something buzzes wrong.

Setting

CMU music technology studio

Shared studio space at CMU — floorspace, carts, and room to test sound, light, and sensory rigs without pretending it is quiet.

Medium

Quartz bowls + circuits

Frosted singing bowls for long decays; microcontrollers and actuators to explore repeatable, gentle strikes.

Aim

Full sensory bath

Slow arcs of tone, light, and vibration — closer to lying on the floor in a studio than chasing a radio single.

Status

Active prototype

We iterate in the open: document listens, revise wiring, and chase gestures that feel kind in the room.

Sonic Plunge is our working title for this build — centered on quartz singing bowls and whatever we wrap around them — speakers, lights, motors, and other sensory paths. The longer story lives up in Concept, Problem, and Approach; down here is the messy bench-level detail.

“Automated” for us still means lots of hand-tuning — it is just that the hand is not always on the mallet. We log strikes, sketch signal paths, and argue about whether a take felt kind before we move on.

♭ Materials & orchestration

  • Acoustic Multiple frosted singing bowls, soft mallets, occasional gong / room mics for reference recordings — our “strings & choir.”
  • Electronics Microcontroller-based control, breadboard circuits, servos or small motors for strike experiments, batteries and regulated power where needed.
  • Light LED accents inside or under bowls to reinforce slow visual rhythm alongside sound.
  • Sensory Tactile transducers, seating vibration, or other physical channels so the low end is not only in your ears — tuned so it supports the bath rather than rattling the room.
  • Software Laptop tools for timing tests, audio capture, and iteration notes — whatever helps us repeat a listen honestly.

𝄐 Timeline

Six steps, roughly in order: what we felt in a real studio, what we learned with our hands, when we started designing, and how the audio side ran next to the build until it all had to meet in one room.

  1. 1 · Experiencing the sound bath at the studio

    We went to an actual sound bath first. That sounds obvious, but it mattered: you can read about bowls all day and still not know what the room is supposed to feel like. Low light, slow movement, sound washing around you — it gave us a shared reference for “this is what we are aiming at,” not just a playlist.

    It also made clear the project was never only about loudness or clarity. The body picks up pressure, vibration, and space as much as the ears do. We carried that into everything after.

  2. 2 · Trying the bowls ourselves

    Back at school we put bowls on the floor and tried to play them the way you are supposed to — soft contact, steady motion, not poking the metal like it owes you money. It was awkward at first, which was useful: you only learn how touchy these things are when your own wrist is doing the work.

    Small changes in angle, speed, or where the mallet lands made a bigger difference than we expected. That fed directly into what we later asked motors to repeat (and what we decided was too finicky to fake).

  3. 3 · Understanding what needed to be automated

    After listening and then playing, we started naming what had to show up in the final piece: long decays, enough silence, a sense of motion in the room, and hardware that could hit the same way twice on a Tuesday night without a human standing over every bowl.

    That was the shift from “we liked a feeling” to “here is what the system is responsible for.” Not every detail became a motor — some stayed in the mix, the lights, or the seating — but we finally had a list we could argue about instead of guessing.

  4. 4 · Building the sound design in parallel

    While the bowl side of the group was still mostly mallets and tape, another thread was the speaker rig: where to put drivers, how wide to paint the stereo field, and how to keep the bowls from feeling like a sticker on top of a track instead of part of the same space.

    This phase was less about “correct” audio and more about shaping the environment — what you notice when you stop looking at your phone and actually lie down for twenty minutes.

  5. 5 · Writing and recording the music

    We sketched material on paper and in the DAW, then recorded passes and listened back in the same room we hoped to install in. Nothing fancy — mostly marking what felt too busy, what needed more air, and which takes still sounded kind after a long day.

    The point was to see how composed sound would sit next to live bowl energy once the physical rig showed up. Some ideas survived; some got cut once we heard them against real strikes.

  6. 6 · Bringing the system together

    Slowly the hardware path, the mixes, and the sensory ideas stopped living in separate chats. We started running partial setups end to end — ugly, sometimes — but enough to tell whether the installation was turning into one thing or three demos in the same room.

    We are still fixing details, but the picture now is a single system: bowls, electronics, speakers, and light all answering to the same rough schedule instead of fighting each other.

𝄌 Coda — what we’re learning

Prototyping together has meant translating half-finished ideas across tables, laptops, and voice memos. We have gotten better at naming what we hear, not just whether it “works” — a small shift that changes how we iterate.

Connecting artistic intention to physical systems is slower than drawing a diagram. The bowls reward care; the electronics reward patience. When those two line up, the room changes in a way that is hard to screenshot.

Testing has been a kind of listening: repeating setups until we trust them, and staying honest when something feels clever but not kind. We are still learning how to carry a full-room listening idea out of imagination and into a real space — one version at a time.

If there is one thing we want this project page to say, it is this: the installation is not only the objects on the floor — it is also sound, light, touch, and the process of listening together until the room feels a little more generous than when we started.
